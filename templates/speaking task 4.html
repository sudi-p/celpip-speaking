Make predictions

Speaking Task 4 — Show your ability to hypothesize, reason logically, and speak confidently about future or imaginary scenarios.

Time to speak
60s
Prep time
30s
Scenario type
Hypothetical
Target band
8–12
What the task involves

You are given a hypothetical scenario and asked to predict what will happen next or how someone will respond. The question might be: "What do you think will happen if this situation continues?" or "Predict how the industry will change in 5 years."

The examiner is assessing your ability to think ahead logically, use conditional language, support predictions with reasoning, and speak with confidence and fluency.

Scoring criteria (what examiners watch for)
Logic Predictions are reasonable and well-supported by evidence or reasoning
Vocabulary Use of conditional, predictive, and speculative language (will, might, could, likely)
Fluency Smooth delivery without excessive hesitation or filler words
Accuracy Proper use of future tense, conditionals, and grammatically correct sentences

Use this 3-part structure to organize your prediction logically — not a script to memorize, but a guide to stay on track.

Part 1: Acknowledge & Setup (10–15 seconds)

Restate or acknowledge the scenario briefly. Show you understood it.

"If someone loses their job suddenly, I think there will be several immediate changes in their life..."

Part 2: Main Predictions (35–40 seconds)

Present 2–3 specific predictions or outcomes. Use conditional and predictive language. Each prediction should be clear and logically connected.

"First, they will likely face financial stress. Second, they might decide to update their resume. Third, they could explore a new career direction."

Part 3: Summary & Reasoning (10–15 seconds)

Briefly recap or add a concluding thought that ties your predictions together.

"Overall, I believe the long-term impact will depend on whether they find support from family and whether the job market improves."
Pro tips
✦ Use conditional language: "will likely," "might," "could," "probably," "I predict that..."
✦ Always support predictions with why — give reasons, not just what will happen
✦ Don't say "I don't know" — even if uncertain, use speculative language: "I imagine," "It's possible that..."
✦ Link predictions to real-world factors (economy, technology, human behavior) to sound credible
✦ Speak at a natural pace — rushed predictions sound unconvincing

10–12 Advanced
Predictions are insightful, well-reasoned, and supported. Uses a range of conditional and predictive structures. Speaks fluently with clear organization. Grammar is nearly always correct. Vocabulary is sophisticated and precise.
7–9 Upper-Intermediate
Predictions are logical and generally clear. Uses conditional and predictive language consistently. Some pauses or hesitations, but overall fluent. Minor grammatical errors. Vocabulary is appropriate and varied.
4–6 Intermediate
Basic predictions are present but sometimes vague. Limited range of conditional language. Noticeable pauses and some filler sounds. Several grammatical errors. Vocabulary is basic and repetitive.

Predictions are unclear or illogical. Little to no use of conditional or predictive language. Frequent hesitations or long silences. Many grammatical errors. Very limited vocabulary.
Prep: Organize your thoughts

In your 30 seconds, write down 2–3 bullet points: each prediction + one reason why. Don't write sentences — just key words. This keeps you on track without sounding like you're reading.

Delivery: Speak with confidence

Even if you're unsure, use confident language: "I predict," "It will," "This would" — not "maybe" or "I think maybe." Examiners reward confident speculation over hesitant certainty.

Language: Use signal phrases

Open with "If this happens, I think..." or "Looking ahead, I predict..." These phrases show you understand the task and use sophisticated grammar.

Logic: Always explain your reasoning

Don't just say what will happen. Say why. "Prices will rise because demand will increase" is better than just "prices will rise."

Prep: Practice with real scenarios

Before the test, practice predicting real events: "If interest rates drop, what happens to house prices?" This builds the habit of logical thinking.

Delivery: Pause between predictions

Use pauses strategically: "First, X will happen. Second, Y will happen." This creates structure and gives you time to breathe, not filler sounds.

Language: Avoid repetition

Don't say "I think" 10 times. Vary: "I predict," "It's likely," "My expectation is," "I would imagine." Vocabulary variety impresses examiners.

Logic: Connect to real-world factors

Reference actual systems (economy, psychology, technology) rather than pure speculation. "The job market will improve because companies are hiring again" sounds smarter than "I think maybe it will be better."
