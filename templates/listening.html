The Listening section is deceptively short. Audio plays once, headphones stay on, and the questions arrive while your brain is still translating the last sentence you heard. Most candidates lose points not because they can't understand English — but because they haven't learned how to listen on a clock.

This guide breaks down each of the six tasks, the traps each one sets, and the listening posture you should walk in with. Tap any task below to expand its strategy notes, scan the rapid tips, then test yourself with the mini-quiz at the end.

PART ONE

The Six Tasks, Explained

Task 01

Listening to Problem Solving

~ 8 questions · 1 conversation · everyday situation
+

You hear a conversation in three parts: someone describes a problem, the listener offers options or asks questions, and finally the speaker chooses a solution and explains why. Questions appear after each part — not all at the end.

How to Approach

01
Part A

Identify who has the problem, what exactly is wrong, and any specifics (dates, names, products).

02
Part B

Track every option the responder mentions. There are usually 3–4 — and one will become the answer in Part C.

03
Part C

Listen for the reason behind the chosen solution — that's almost always a question.

Common Traps

  • Distractor answers repeat exact words from the audio — but in the wrong context.
  • The speaker may reject an option after first sounding interested in it.
  • Numbers (prices, room numbers, dates) are often paraphrased.

What to Note Mentally

  • Each option discussed + a one-word reason it was kept or dropped.
  • Reversal words: "actually," "but," "on second thought."
  • The final choice and the justification ("because…").
If you only remember one thing for Task 1: the answer often hinges on why a solution was chosen — not just what was chosen.

Rapid Tips

1

Don't pre-read in Task 1 — questions appear one at a time, mid-conversation. Stay focused on the audio.

2

Eliminate answers using opposite logic: if two options say opposite things, one is usually the trap.

3

Watch for tone shifts — hesitation ("hmm, well…") usually signals rejection of an option.

Task 02

Listening to a Daily Life Conversation

~ 5 questions · 1 dialogue · two speakers
+

A casual exchange — friends, family, neighbours, coworkers chatting. The relationship and the emotional content matter as much as the facts.

How to Approach

01
Setting

Decide instantly: who are these two, and what's their relationship?

02
Topic

The first 20 seconds almost always reveal the central topic — anchor everything else to it.

03
Feelings

Note each speaker's attitude — happy, frustrated, surprised, sceptical.

04
Outcome

What do they agree to do, or what's left unresolved?

Common Traps

  • Idioms and slang ("a piece of cake," "spill the beans") — translate the meaning, not the words.
  • Sarcasm is rare but does appear. Tone tells you everything.
  • Questions ask about inference, not literal lines.

What to Note

  • Mood words: "annoyed," "excited," "worried."
  • Action verbs in the future tense — these often map to questions.
  • Names and roles (Sarah = sister, Mark = manager).
In Task 2, the feeling behind the words is the answer.

Rapid Tips

1

Practice with podcasts and sitcoms — How I Met Your Mother or The Office (US) mirror the register.

2

If an option uses an extreme word like "never" or "always," be sceptical — daily-life answers are usually moderate.

3

Train your ear for connected speech: "whaddya," "gonna," "kinda" — these aren't typos, they're real CELPIP English.

Task 03

Listening for Information

~ 6 questions · long monologue or interview · detail-heavy
+

A long, factual conversation or interview — usually about a place, programme, study, or service. This task is the densest with names, dates, and numbers.

How to Approach

01
Pre-Read

You DO get time to read questions before audio plays. Use every second — circle keywords mentally.

02
Predict

From the questions, guess the topic and what kind of facts you'll need (a date? a place? a job title?).

03
Track

Mentally tally specifics as they come: numbers, percentages, locations.

04
Move On

If you miss one detail, do not dwell. The next question is already coming.

Common Traps

  • The audio mentions a number that sounds right but applies to a different thing entirely.
  • "Up to 50%" and "exactly 50%" are different answers.
  • Names of people, places, or programmes get rapidly paraphrased.

What to Note

  • Sequence words: "first," "later," "after that" — order matters.
  • Cause/effect markers: "due to," "because of," "as a result."
  • Quantifiers: "most," "some," "few," "many."
Task 3 rewards active prediction. Read the question, picture the answer, then listen for confirmation.

Rapid Tips

1

Practice with CBC Radio, BBC News, TED Talks — same factual register, similar speed.

2

When two answers are numerically close, the speaker usually gives a hint with "about," "roughly," "exactly," or "more than." That hint is the answer.

3

If a question asks about a future plan, listen for "will," "going to," "we're planning to."

Task 04

Listening to a News Item

~ 5 questions · short broadcast · journalistic tone
+

A short news report — usually 60–90 seconds. Fast, formal, packed with facts. The structure mimics real news: headline → details → context → quote → consequence.

How to Approach

01
Headline

The first sentence is the story's spine. Lock onto it immediately.

02
5 W's

Who, What, Where, When, Why — the news structure gives all five within a minute.

03
Quotes

Statements from officials or witnesses often appear in questions verbatim.

04
Consequence

"As a result…" or "This means…" — the final lines often answer the last question.

Common Traps

  • News audio uses passive voice heavily ("was announced," "is being investigated") — don't lose track of who did what.
  • Numbers come fast and back-to-back. Mentally group them ("$2M = investment, 30 = workers").

What to Note

  • Source attributions: "according to," "officials say."
  • Time references: "yesterday," "this morning," "later this week."
  • Reactions: "the public has criticized," "experts warn."
News reports follow a pyramid. The top is everything — listen hardest in the first 15 seconds.

Rapid Tips

1

Listen to CBC Radio One news bulletins daily. The cadence is identical to Task 4 audio.

2

Be wary of options that mention a real fact from the report but answer a different question.

3

Tone in news is neutral. Options that sound emotional or biased are usually wrong.

Task 05

Listening to a Discussion

~ 8 questions · video clip · three speakers, multiple viewpoints
+

This is the only task where you see video. Two or three speakers debate or discuss a topic. The challenge: keeping track of who said what and who agrees with whom.

How to Approach

01
Faces

Look at the video — names and faces are paired. Mentally tag each speaker.

02
Positions

Identify each speaker's stance in their first 10 seconds. For/against/neutral?

03
Agreement

Watch for "I agree, but…" — partial agreement is often tested.

04
Examples

Each speaker usually gives a personal example. These are gold for the questions.

Common Traps

  • Quoting the wrong speaker — questions ask "What does Maria think?" not what was said in general.
  • One speaker may shift their view mid-discussion. Catch the shift.
  • Visuals can distract — eyes on the speaker, ears on the content.

What to Note

  • Name → one-word stance ("Tom = pro, Lisa = skeptical").
  • Concession words: "however," "still," "even so," "that said."
  • Group-vs-individual claims.
In a discussion, who said it matters as much as what was said.

Rapid Tips

1

The video isn't decoration — body language and head nods can confirm agreement.

2

Practice with panel shows like The Agenda with Steve Paikin or Q&A debates.

3

If you genuinely can't remember who said something, eliminate the most extreme option — moderate speakers usually win this round.

Task 06

Listening to Viewpoints

~ 6 questions · long monologue · opinion + reasoning
+

One speaker, one extended argument (about 2–3 minutes). They state a position, support it with reasons, address counter-arguments, and conclude. This is the most academic task in the section.

How to Approach

01
Thesis

The speaker's central claim is usually stated in the first 30 seconds. Lock it down.

02
Reasons

Expect 2–3 supporting points. Mark them by their signal words ("firstly," "another reason").

03
Counter

"Some people argue…" introduces opposition — questions love this section.

04
Conclusion

The final line often paraphrases the thesis — and matches an answer choice.

Common Traps

  • The speaker's opinion is asked about, not yours. Stay neutral.
  • Counter-arguments are often misread as the speaker's view. Distinguish carefully.
  • Words like "should," "must," and "ought to" reveal stance.

What to Note

  • Thesis statement in your own words.
  • 2–3 reasons stated, in order.
  • One concession + the speaker's rebuttal.
A viewpoint task is an essay read aloud. Find the thesis, count the body paragraphs, and watch for the conclusion.

Rapid Tips

1

Practice with TED Talks on familiar topics — the structure is identical.

2

If a question asks about tone or purpose, focus on the speaker's adjectives — "alarming," "promising," "outdated" all leak the stance.

3

The conclusion is almost always tested. Don't tune out in the last 20 seconds.

PART TWO

General Strategies Across All Tasks

Headphones Check

The first thing on test day. Adjust volume during the practice task — once Task 1 starts, you can't change it. Set it slightly louder than feels natural.

One Listen Only

There is no replay. No "go back" button. Train at home by listening to audio once and answering — never twice.

Mental Notes, Not Paper

You won't write much. Build the habit of holding 3–4 key facts in working memory while listening.

The 5-Second Rule

If you don't know an answer in 5 seconds, guess and move on. The next question is already loading.

Paraphrase Awareness

Correct answers almost never reuse the audio's exact wording. If an option matches verbatim, suspect it.

Accent Familiarity

CELPIP uses Canadian English. Expect "about" (not "aboot"), "process," "schedule" with sk-, and everyday Canadian references.

Predict Before You Hear

When you get pre-reading time, use it to guess the answer type, not memorize options. You'll listen sharper.

Manage the Clock

Each question has a soft timer. Don't get stuck — every second on Q3 is borrowed from Q4.

PART THREE

Common Mistakes & How to Dodge Them

Mistake 01

Trying to understand every word

+

You don't need to. CELPIP is testing whether you grasp meaning, not vocabulary. If you miss a word, keep moving — context will usually fill the gap. Obsessing over one unknown word is how candidates miss the next three sentences.

Mistake 02

Choosing the option that sounds familiar

+

Familiarity is a trap. The test designs distractors that echo the audio without matching its meaning. Always ask: "Does this option answer the question that was actually asked?"

Mistake 03

Ignoring the pre-reading window

+

You're given a few seconds to skim questions before some tasks. Most candidates stare at the screen blankly. Instead, scan keywords and predict the answer type — this can shift your score by a full band.

Mistake 04

Panicking after one missed question

+

Every test-taker misses a few. Panic only spreads the damage. Treat each question as independent — the previous one no longer exists.
