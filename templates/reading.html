Reading Correspondence

Email or letter · 11 questions · ~11 minutes
What you'll see11 questions

A message, then a reply.

You read an email or letter, answer 6 questions about it, then fill in 5 blanks in a short reply.

  1. Read the opening lines first. The sender, recipient, and subject reveal the situation — who is writing to whom, and why. This frames every question that follows.
  2. Skim the whole email once at normal pace before touching the questions. Don't try to memorize — you're building a mental map of where things live.
  3. Read each question carefully, then scan back. Don't answer from memory. Locate the exact paragraph that contains the answer and re-read those 2–3 sentences.
  4. For the reply (Part B), read the entire reply first to feel the tone and direction. Then go blank by blank — each blank usually has 4 options that test grammar, vocabulary, or logical flow.
  5. Plug each option in mentally and ask: does the sentence still flow naturally? Eliminate options that break grammar or contradict the original email.
Tone Match
The reply must echo the tone of the original. Formal email = formal reply. A friendly note doesn't suddenly become "Dear Sir/Madam."
Watch Connectors
"However," "therefore," "in addition" — these almost always test logical relationships. Read the sentence before AND after the blank.
Pronoun Anchors
When you see "this," "they," or "it" — find what it refers to in the previous sentence. Questions often test this.
Implication ≠ Statement
Questions like "What can be inferred?" want you to read between the lines. The literal answer is usually a trap.
Mark & Move
Stuck for 45+ seconds? Flag, guess your best option, keep going. You can return if time allows within the part.
Vocabulary in Context
If a word is unfamiliar, look at the surrounding sentence. Synonyms or contrast words usually reveal meaning.
Common Pitfalls
  • Choosing an answer because the words "match" the passage — CELPIP rewards meaning, not word-spotting.
  • Skipping the subject line or sign-off (they often hint at relationship and purpose).
  • Filling all reply blanks in one pass without re-reading the surrounding sentences.
  • Letting one tricky question eat 3 minutes — you'll lose easier marks at the end.

Reading to Apply a Diagram

Diagram + email · 8 questions · ~9 minutes
What you'll see8 questions

Visual on the left, message on the right.

A schedule, brochure, map, or product comparison sits beside a short email asking for advice based on that diagram.

  1. Look at the diagram first. Understand the structure — categories, headings, dates, prices, conditions. Read every footnote and asterisk; that's where exceptions hide.
  2. Then read the email. Note the writer's constraints: budget, dates available, food allergies, kids, accessibility — these are filters.
  3. For each question, return to the diagram with the constraint in mind. Don't trust memory; the diagram is dense and details are precise.
  4. Cross-reference systematically. If the question says "Which option suits a family of four with a dog?" — scan rows for both criteria, not one.
  5. Trust the diagram over assumptions. If the brochure says "no pets allowed on weekends," that overrides any common-sense guess.
Star the Footnotes
Asterisks and small print almost always become a question. Read them before you start answering.
Underline Constraints
In the email, mentally circle every number, date, condition, and preference. These are your filters.
One Variable at a Time
Eliminate options by checking one constraint at a time. It feels slower but stops careless errors.
Beware "All of the Above"
If two answers seem right, re-check the diagram — usually one violates a constraint you missed.
Pace: 1 Min/Question
9 minutes for 8 questions. If you're spending 90+ seconds, mark and move.
Same-Day Differences
Weekday vs weekend pricing, peak vs off-peak — CELPIP loves to test this distinction.
Common Pitfalls
  • Spending too long reading the diagram top to bottom — scan it like a chart, not prose.
  • Forgetting a constraint from the email by question 5. Re-anchor before each question.
  • Picking the option that "sounds best" instead of the one that fits the writer's specific situation.
  • Missing exceptions in fine print (closed Mondays, requires booking, members only).

Reading for Information

4-paragraph article · 9 questions · ~13 minutes
What you'll see9 questions

One long passage. Four labeled paragraphs.

An informational article (often about Canadian history, science, society) where each question asks: "Which paragraph contains this idea?" plus a few specific-detail questions.

  1. Skim each paragraph for its main idea before reading questions. Jot a 2–3 word label in your head: "early history," "modern impact," "economic effects," "criticism."
  2. Read the questions, identify the keyword in each. Is it about a date? A cause? A consequence? A person's view?
  3. Match keywords to your paragraph labels. If a question asks about economic consequences, you already know which paragraph to check.
  4. Confirm with the actual text. Don't pick a paragraph just because a word appears there — make sure the idea matches.
  5. "Not given" is rare here but possible. If you can't find support in any paragraph after careful scanning, the answer is often the option that paraphrases an idea most accurately.
Label Paragraphs
Give each paragraph a one-word mental tag. You'll match questions to tags in seconds.
Paraphrase Detection
Answers rarely copy text word-for-word. Look for synonyms: "began" = "originated," "harmed" = "had adverse effects."
Beware Word Traps
A paragraph might mention "economic" once but not be about economics. Read the surrounding sentence to confirm.
Topic Sentences
First and last sentences of each paragraph usually contain the main idea. Re-read these when stuck.
Numbers & Dates Lie
If a date appears in two paragraphs, check what it refers to in each — the question wants a specific event.
Pace: ~1.4 Min/Q
13 minutes ÷ 9 questions, minus skim time. Aim for 60–80 seconds per question.
Common Pitfalls
  • Reading the entire passage in detail before looking at questions — wastes 4+ minutes.
  • Picking a paragraph because a single word matches the question. Confirm the idea, not the word.
  • Confusing cause with effect — read carefully when questions ask "why" vs "what happened."
  • Forgetting that one paragraph may answer multiple questions (and one may answer none).

Reading for Viewpoints

Opinion article + reader comment · 10 questions · ~13 minutes
What you'll see10 questions

An article with multiple opinions. Then a reader comment with blanks.

A newspaper-style article presents 2–3 viewpoints on an issue. Five questions follow. Then a reader's response has 5 blanks to fill — testing whether you grasped the perspectives.

  1. Identify the speakers and their positions as you read. Who supports the policy? Who opposes? Who is neutral? Mentally tag each one.
  2. Note the writer's own stance. The article's author may be neutral, supportive, or skeptical — questions often test this.
  3. For Part A questions, match each question to the right viewpoint. "What does the researcher believe?" — go to the researcher's quote, not the critic's.
  4. For the reader comment (Part B), read the whole comment first to see whether the reader agrees, disagrees, or partly agrees with someone in the article.
  5. Fill blanks using viewpoint logic. The blank options will all be grammatical — the right one captures the reader's specific opinion and matches the surrounding sentences.
Map the Voices
Quickly note who says what: Person A = pro, Person B = con, Person C = cautious. Crucial for accuracy.
Identify the Reader's Side
Before answering blanks, decide: does the reader agree with the writer? With which expert? This frames every blank.
Hedge Words Matter
"Might," "could," "tends to" signal qualified opinions. "Clearly," "always," "must" signal strong positions.
Implicit Bias
2025 updates emphasize spotting unstated bias — what the author chooses to include, emphasize, or omit.
Watch the Connectors
In the reader comment, words before each blank ("but," "moreover," "in contrast") direct the answer's logic.
Tone of the Reader
A frustrated reader picks emotional words. A measured reader uses cautious ones. Match the register.
Common Pitfalls
  • Attributing one expert's view to another — mix-ups happen when speakers blur together.
  • Assuming the writer agrees with the first expert quoted. They often don't.
  • Picking blank answers based only on grammar — all four options usually fit grammatically.
  • Missing sarcasm or skepticism in the reader comment ("Oh, sure, that'll definitely work").

Cross-Task Strategy

Time, pacing, preparation

Pre-Test Checklist — tap to mark done

Take at least 3 full-length timed practice tests to internalize pacing.
Read one Canadian news article daily (CBC, Globe and Mail) — builds context and vocabulary.
Practice scanning skills: find a name, date, or fact in a long article within 20 seconds.
Learn 50 high-frequency academic connectors (however, moreover, consequently, etc.).
Drill paraphrase recognition — practice spotting synonyms and rewordings.
Familiarize yourself with the official CELPIP interface — the digital reading experience.
On test day: pen, paper, water, ID, and arrive 30 min early.
Sleep 7+ hours the night before. Reading needs a fresh brain more than any other section.
